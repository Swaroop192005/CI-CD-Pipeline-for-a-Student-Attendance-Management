### Reading those numbers honestly

**The 19 Selenium journeys contribute nothing to this figure.** They drive
a browser against a container running in a separate JVM, which the
coverage agent is not attached to. So the real coverage of the application
is higher than 67.9%; this measures only what 110 of the 129 tests reach.

That is also why `web` reads 27.3%. `CaseController` shows 7.8% and
`SessionController` 9.2%, yet both are exercised end to end by journeys
J3 and J6. The controllers are tested; the measurement cannot see it.

**`service` at 85.8% is the number that matters.** The business rules live
there -- the attendance percentage, BR-03 excusing, the two case tiers,
session opening and closing. That is the code where a silent mistake
changes a student's eligibility.

**Branch coverage at 49.5% is the weakest figure and is not explained
away.** It means roughly half the conditional paths are untried. Much of
that is error handling and defensive branches, but "much of" is not "all
of", and branch coverage is the more honest metric of the two.

**Genuinely untested:** `PasskeyAssertionService` at 16.1%. Its refusals
were verified by driving the running application -- a replayed challenge,
a credential belonging to another account -- but those checks live in a
scratch script, not in the suite. It is the clearest gap.

## Notes on the journeys

J3 and J6 hold few `@Test` methods but are the longest-running: each walks
a complete multi-role journey with dozens of assertions inside one test,
signing in and out as faculty, student and head of department in turn.
Counting methods understates them.

The journeys assume a freshly seeded instance, which the pipeline provides
by deploying a new container each run. They are not re-runnable against an
instance they have already written to: a journey that creates a record is
refused the second time by the uniqueness rule it relies on. That failure
is the fixture, not the application.
